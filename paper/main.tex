\begin{abstract}
This paper examines the impact of improvement in world market access on urbanization and agricultural specialization. I employ a 2SLS framework, utilizing the predicted road network from the Plan for National Integration (PIN) devised during the military dictatorship as a source of exogenous variation. The findings confirm a larger increase in urbanization level and specialization in transport-cost-sensitive agricultural commodities for micro-regions with larger reductions in travel time to the world market. The long-differenced estimates show that a 10\% reduction in travel time corresponds to a 2.7\% increase in GDP and 3.3\% increase in urban population. Event study estimates reveal a persistent effect, with the expansion of transport-cost-sensitive soybeans closely following the road construction, whereas the impact on less sensitive cattle is unclear. Heterogeneity in effects is mainly reflected in the value of new infrastructure in a particular micro-region. These results are in line with the prediction of a Ricardian trade model, providing empirical evidence for a spatial Balassa-Samuelson mechanism that the increase in the tradable (rural) sector productivity translates into a growth in the non-tradable (urban) sector.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The relationship between economic growth and trade is central to the fields of both international and development economics. A well-connected road network is essential for fostering both domestic and international trade. However, many rapidly developing countries, such as India, China, and Brazil, face significant constraints on their transportation infrastructure. To what extent do trade cost reductions brought by road construction promote urbanization and economic development? What are the distributional effects of international trade shocks? What role do internal trade costs play in the transmission of the shocks?

This paper seeks to provide new reduced-form evidence to answer these questions, using Brazil's significant variation in internal integration following the Plan for National Integration (PIN) in 1970 as a natural experiment. The project called for the construction of over 20,000 km of highways to assert control over the Amazon. This empirical setting has several desirable features for examining the relationship between economic development and international trade. First, Brazil's integration into the world economy was fueled by the trade cost reductions induced by PIN. Implemented during Brazil's military dictatorship 20 years before the trade liberalization in 1990, this plan aimed to strengthen national security and hence is plausibly exogenous to the economic characteristics of Brazil's peripheral locations. Second, the expansion of the actual road network from 1970 to 2010 significantly lowered inland transport costs, especially for the previously underdeveloped Amazon, allowing for an examination of the role of internal integration in shaping development patterns. Third, the availability of disaggregated data on economic activities across regions in Brazil over 40 years enables the quantification of the structural transformation and expansion pattern of tradable commodities.

To address the concerns of nonrandom road placements, I exploit the transport cost reductions induced by the PIN road network as an instrument for the actual reduction. I construct a decadal panel dataset from 1970 to 2010, combining microregion-level characteristics, travel time to the closest international port on the actual road network, and outcome variables including GDP, population, urban population, urban population share, agricultural employment, and production. Additionally, the data are aggregated into a cross-section of the difference between outcomes and travel time from 1970 to 2010.

The analysis proceeds as follows. To guide the empirical investigation, I adapt a Ricardian trade model from Fajgelbaum and Redding (2022), which formalizes the role of transport costs in shaping the growth and distribution of economic activities across locations. The model has two central predictions for the impact of transport costs. First, the model establishes a spatial Balassa-Samuelson effect to capture the relationship between structural transformation, economic development, and international trade. Locations with larger reductions in trade costs will see larger growth in GDP, population, and urbanization. Second, locations with larger reductions in transport costs will specialize more in the transport-cost-sensitive commodities.

I find strong empirical support for these predictions. My baseline specification is a long-differenced estimation that controls for long-run trends during the 1970-2010 period. Using the newly constructed spatially disaggregated dataset, I find that over the 40-year period, locations with 10\% reductions in transport costs to world markets have a 2.7\% more increase in GDP and a 3.3\% more increase in urban population. I further examine the timing-varying effects of the reduction in travel time on outcomes of interest in an event study design. Dynamic estimates confirm that there are no pre-trends in population growth but a sudden spike in urban population from 1980 onwards for those micro-regions predicted to experience larger reductions in travel time to ports. Furthermore, the results reveal that locations with 10\% more reductions in transport costs experience 36.3\% more production in transport-cost-sensitive soybeans, while no significant increases are found in insensitive cattle production. I employ a probability model to uncover the expansion pattern of the agricultural production and confirm that such expansion in soybeans is driven by both the extensive and intensive margins. Additional heterogeneity analysis reveals that the effect of transport cost reductions is more pronounced in micro-regions where the newly built infrastructure is particularly valuable, i.e., regions with worse access to waterways, located in the Center-West, and farther away from the state capital benefit more from the road construction. The main results survive a battery of robustness checks, including alternative robust estimators and alternative specifications and sample choices.

This paper relates to several bodies of literature. First, a rich set of spatial literature on the pattern of migration assumed that workers are immobile across space (Donaldson, 2018; Topalova, 2010), or migration costs are purely due to preferences without frictions (Allen and Arkolakis, 2014; Redding, 2016). Recent literature relaxes these assumptions to consider costly migration (Tombe and Zhu, 2019; Morten and Oliveria, 2024). I model imperfect labor mobility through idiosyncratic location preferences, so that population responds to spatial real-income differences with a finite migration elasticity, and focus on the role of transport costs separately on migration, trade, and economic growth relative to other papers considering an interaction between them. Second, this paper is related to research on economic geography and market access, including Hanson (1996), Venables and Limão (2002), Redding and Sturm (2008), Allen and Arkolakis (2014), Coşar and Fajgelbaum (2016), Redding (2016), Sotelo (2020), Fajgelbaum and Redding (2022), and Morten and Oliveira (2024). Third, a growing empirical literature has examined the relationship between economic activity and transport infrastructure, including Chandra and Thompson (2000), Baum-Snow (2007), Michaels (2008), Berlinski, Galiani, and Jaitman (2011), Duranton and Turner (2012), Duranton, Morrow, and Turner (2014), Faber (2014), Atkin and Donaldson (2015), Coşar and Demir (2016), Storeygard (2016), Baum-Snow et al. (2017), Donaldson (2018), Pérez (2018), Banerjee, Duflo, and Qian (2020), and Heblich, Redding, and Sturm (2020), as reviewed in Redding and Turner (2015). Last, I build on the economic and historical literature on Brazil's economic development, including Bird and Straub (2014), Pellegrina (2014), Pellegrina and Sotelo (2021), Pellegrina (2022), and Soled et al. (2024). This paper aims to investigate the role of road access in promoting agricultural production and urbanization through the lens of trade, with a specific focus on the production of disaggregated agricultural commodities in Brazil.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 discusses the historical background in Brazil between 1970 and 2010. Section 3 develops the theoretical model and derives the two key empirical predictions. Sections 4 and 5 discuss the data and identification strategy. Section 6 presents the main empirical results. Section 7 performs several robustness checks to validate the results. Section 8 interprets the results based on the theoretical model and discusses potential concerns. Section 9 concludes.


\section{Historical Context}
\subsection{Plan for National Integration (1970)}
The Legal Amazon, designated in 1948, comprises all seven states in Brazil’s North Region, most of Mato Grosso, and part of Maranhão. In 2010, the region accounted for about 10\% of the population and 8.1\% of GDP (IBGE, 2010). However, for most of the 20th century, the region was largely unoccupied and sparsely populated. Before the 1970s, the main transportation in the region was carried through the waterways with minimal road connection in between. 

Brazil started their transportation infrastructure construction sporadically in the late 19th century. In 1964, Brazil became a military dictatorship. The valorization of the country's territorial attributes was accompanied by an increase in its defensive capacity. The need for a more effective occupation of the Amazon Rainforest was prioritized. One of the most decisive infrastructure construction projects was the Plan for National Integration (PIN) launched in 1970, which called for the construction of nearly 20,000 km of highway systems, including the Trans-Amazonia Highway, Cuiaba-Santarem Highway, and Northern Perimeter Highway with a network of feeder roads. Under the slogan “integrate to not surrender,” the government urged placing the stamp of Brazilian settlement on Amazonia so that Brazilian sovereignty would never be contested (Fearnside 1984, 47). The goal of PIN was twofold: firstly, to connect state capitals in the Legal Amazon with other regions in Brazil to assert control over the Amazon and thereby strengthen national sovereignty; secondly, to reduce the flux of migrants to the populated coastal metropolitans. The National Transportation Plan (PNV) in 1973, shaped by PIN's planning documents, is presented in Figure 1.

Construction was only partially completed. By 1974, with rising social conflict and limited capacity, the PIN plan was canceled. Despite this, much of Brazil’s current freight movement—about 60\%—relies on infrastructure built during this era (Bottasso et al., 2021). PNV 1973 provided rights-of-way facilitating lower-cost highway construction or pavement over or alongside old roads, most of which has taken place since 1990.

\subsection{Trade Liberalization (1990s)}
Brazil was heavily protected and imposed trade restrictions to address balance of payment difficulties despite being one of the founding members of the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade. After the transition from a military to a civilian government in 1985, Brazil embarked on a major trade liberalization process driven by the macroeconomic and debt crises in many Latin American economies during the 1980s.

The trade reform broke the nearly 100-year-high barriers to trade. The average nominal tariffs were unilaterally lowered from 54.9\% in 1987 to 10.5\% in 1994. During the 1988-1989 period, tariff redundancy, special regimes, and additional taxes were partially removed. In March 1990, the newly elected president unexpectedly eliminated non-tariff trade barriers, and the trade liberalization effectively started. The trade reform in tariffs culminated in 1995 with the establishment of Mercosur, a multilateral common market to promote the free movement of goods, services, and productive factors between countries.

In July of 1994, the Brazilian government implemented a new macroeconomic stabilization plan called Plano Real to curb inflation. A prominent feature was the adoption of a crawling-peg exchange rate system in order to restore the public's confidence in the domestic currency. This plan was not successful in keeping government budget deficits under control and undermined the government's ability to sustain the crawling-peg system. In January of 1999, an unexpected and large speculative attack on the domestic currency left the Brazilian government with no other choice than to abandon the crawling peg and adopt a free-floating exchange rate system. A large real exchange rate devaluation of approximately 50\% then ensued, which, however, proves to further heighten incentives for farmers to increase their output of internationally traded commodities with prices in Brazilian reais approaching historical heights (Richards et al., 2012). This further boosted exports and incentivized agricultural expansion and investment in the region.

\subsection{Agriculture to Growth}
Brazil has emerged as a top global exporter of soybeans, cotton, and meat. Soybeans have been pivotal in the growth of Brazil's agricultural sector and its rise as a leading global agricultural exporter. Commercial soybean cultivation began in the 1940s and was solidified in the southern states during the 1960s, aided by industrial support under the military regime indirectly benefiting agricultural commodities.

Post-liberalization, the soybean complex spread to the Cerrado and Amazon, gradually replacing the South. By 2005, Mato Grosso had become the leading soybean-exporting state. Such conversion of previous range, pasture, and other land into cropland was driven by both rising international demand and supply factors: technological development, which made feasible the cultivation in formerly inhospitable areas, and the investment in infrastructure, which greatly reduced transportation costs (Mueller, 2003).

Such supply-side drivers laid the groundwork for the agricultural establishment in the West. First, the National Agricultural Research Institution developed new seed varieties that thrived under the diverse and challenging tropical conditions, making previously unsuitable low-latitude areas viable for soybean cultivation. Second, pavement of previously constructed PIN, like the Cuiaba-Santarem Highway, has significantly enhanced connectivity by linking the Cerrado directly to the ports along the Amazon River in Santarém, which offers a logistical advantage for the region’s substantial agricultural outputs over the distant Port of Santos. The improvement in infrastructure has dramatically reduced travel times during the rainy season from weeks to hours, improving market access and accommodating the growing demand for soybeans.

\section{Theoretical Model}

In this section, I adapt the theoretical model from Fajgelbaum and Redding (2022) into the Brazillian setting. The model is developed based on a Ricardian Trade model (Eaton and Kortum, 2002) to capture the distribution of economic activities within Brazil across sectors and locations.
The purpose of this section is not to provide a full structural quantitative framework, but rather to derive three key empirical predictions to guide the empirical analysis: first, locations with larger reductions in internal trade costs to world markets experience greater structural transformation toward non-tradable activity; second, such locations specialize in transport-cost-sensitive agricultural goods; third, internal and external integration are complements at the extensive margin, so that reductions in internal trade costs open a location to trade only when the trade regime is sufficiently liberal.

Consider a small open economy with a set of locations $\ell \in L$. Each location is endowed with a fixed amount of land $L_\ell$ and a mobile labor force. There are three sectors: agriculture ($A$), manufacturing ($M$), and nontradables ($N$). Agriculture and manufacturing are tradable, and nontradables are locally consumed.  Given the dominance of Brazilian exports in agricultural products, I focus on the empirically relevant case in which all locations in Brazil have a comparative advantage in agriculture relative to the rest of the world.

Within agriculture, there is a set of disaggregated agricultural goods indexed by $g = 1,\dots,G$ to account for the observed differences in the composition of agricultural production across locations. Each location's land consists of a continuum of land plots $j\in[0,L_{\ell}]$ that are heterogeneous in productivity for disaggregated agricultural goods $g=1,..., G$. 

Each location is connected through an internal road network to international markets and transport cost changes over time with the expansion of road network. Let $\delta_{g(\ell,\ell^*)}=e^{\tau_g t_\ell}\geq 1$ denote the iceberg transport cost between location $\ell$ and the closest international gateway $\ell^*$ for good $g$: shipping one unit between $\ell$ and $\ell^*$ delivers $1/\delta_{g(\ell,\ell^*)}$ units, where $\tau_g>0$ is the heterogeneous transport-cost sensitivity for each good and $t_\ell$ is the common travel time term from location $\ell$ to the closest international port. The transport cost for manufactures is defined analogously as $\delta_{M(\ell,\ell^*)}=e^{\tau_M t_\ell}$ with $\tau_M>0$. I abstract from internal bilateral trade in disaggregated agricultural goods and focus on the case in which local prices are ultimately pinned down through no-arbitrage by exogenous world prices at the closest international gateway ($\{P_g^\ast\}_{g=1}^{G}, P_M^\ast$) and internal transport costs.

\paragraph{A. Preferences}~\\

\noindent Workers' preferences $b_\ell u_\ell$ for each location consist of a common component that is the same for all workers and an idiosyncratic component.

The common component has two tiers: Workers consume tradable and non-tradable goods with constant elasticity of substitution (CES) preferences
\begin{equation}
    u_{\ell} = \left[ \beta_T^{\frac{1}{\sigma}} C_{T\ell}^{\frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma}} + (1 - \beta_T)^{\frac{1}{\sigma}} C_{N\ell}^{\frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma}} \right]^{\frac{\sigma}{\sigma-1}},
    \qquad 0<\sigma<1.
\label{eq:utility}
\end{equation}
where $\beta_T \in (0,1)$ controls the relative weights of tradable and non-tradable goods. I assume inelastic demand between sectors ($0<\sigma<1$) as in the macroeconomics literature on structural transformation. 

Tradables consumption is defined over the manufacturing good and the set of disaggregated agricultural goods with Cobb-Douglas preferences:
\begin{equation}
    C_{T\ell}= C_{M\ell}^{\,1-\gamma_A}\prod_{g=1}^G C_{g\ell}^{\gamma_g},
    \qquad
    \sum_{g=1}^G \gamma_g = \gamma_A \in (0,1),
    \label{eq:tradables_nest}
\end{equation}
where $\gamma_g$ is the expenditure share of agricultural good $g$ within tradables and $\gamma_A$ is the total agricultural expenditure share within tradables. The corresponding tradables price index is, up to a multiplicative constant,
\begin{equation}
        E_{T\ell}= P_{M\ell}^{\,1-\gamma_A}\prod_{g=1}^G P_{g\ell}^{\gamma_g}.
    \label{eq:ET}
\end{equation}
 
The idiosyncratic component $b_\ell$ captures heterogeneity in workers' tastes for locations drawn independently from a Fréchet distribution with shape parameter $\epsilon>1$.
\begin{equation}
    b_\ell \sim \text{Prob}[b_{\ell}<b] = \exp(-b^{-\epsilon})
\end{equation}

Workers first observe their idiosyncratic tastes for each location within Brazil and choose a location to live in by maximizing their utility. Given that the maximum of Fréchet is also Fréchet distributed, the probability that $\ell =\operatorname{argmax}_{\ell} b_\ell u_\ell $ is given by
\begin{equation}
    \pi_\ell=\frac{u_\ell^\epsilon}{\sum_\ell u_\ell^\epsilon}
\end{equation}

Conditional on the exogenous total supply of labor $N$ within Brazil, labor allocated to location $\ell$ is
\begin{equation}
N_\ell = N \left(\frac{u_\ell}{u^*}\right)^{\epsilon},
\qquad
u^* = \left( \sum_{\ell \in L} u_\ell^\epsilon \right)^{1/\epsilon},
\label{eq:labor_supply}
\end{equation}
where $u^*$ is proportional to workers' expected utility, $E[\max_\ell b_\ell u_\ell]=\Gamma(1-1/\epsilon)\,u^*$, which is finite given $\epsilon>1$, and $\epsilon$ controls the elasticity of population with respect to utility. As the variance of the Fréchet distribution decreases as $\epsilon$ increases, i.e., a larger Fréchet shape parameter $\epsilon$ implies greater internal labor mobility within Brazil, such that the supply of labor to each location is more responsive to relative utility levels in goods consumption. In density form,
\begin{equation}
n_\ell L_\ell = N \left(\frac{u_\ell}{u^*}\right)^{\epsilon},
\label{eq:density_supply}
\end{equation}
where $n_\ell \equiv N_\ell/L_\ell$ is population density.


\paragraph{B. Production} ~\\

\noindent Each worker is endowed with one unit of labor supplied inelastically with zero disutility. Land is owned by immobile landowners who do not own any labor. Production in each sector is characterized by a Cobb-Douglas technology with constant returns to scale in land and labor. Output per unit of land in non-tradable and for an agricultural good $g$ in land plot $j$ in location $\ell$ are
\begin{align}
    q_{N\ell} = z_{N\ell} n_{N\ell}^{1-\alpha_N}, \quad
    q_{M\ell} = z_{M\ell} n_{M\ell}^{1-\alpha_M}, \quad
    q_{g\ell j} = z_{g\ell j} n_{g\ell j}^{1-\alpha_A},
\end{align}
where $z_{N\ell},z_{M\ell}, z_{g\ell j}$ are sectoral productivities; $0<\alpha_i<1$ is the land intensity in sector $i=A, M, N$ such that agriculture is more land intensive than non-tradables ($\alpha_A > \alpha_N >0$). For tractability, I assume the two traded sectors share a common land intensity ($\alpha_M = \alpha_A$), as in Coşar and Fajgelbaum (2016). \footnote{Fajgelbaum and Redding (2022) instead assume $\alpha_N<\alpha_M<\alpha_A$, under which complete specialization of traded production in agriculture obtains for sufficiently high agricultural productivity. Setting $\alpha_M=\alpha_A$ delivers the same specialization pattern while yielding a closed-form trade frontier in Section 3.E.}

Land plots differ in productivity for disaggregated agricultural goods drawn from the following Fr\'echet distribution with a shape parameter $\theta$ \footnote{Note that this Fr\'echet distribution has a different shape parameter than the distribution of idiosyncratic taste.}:
\begin{equation}
\Pr\!\left[z_{g\ell j}(\ell)\le z\right]
=
\exp\!\left(-T_{g\ell}\, z^{-\theta}\right),
\qquad \theta>0,
\label{eq:frechet_prod}
\end{equation}
where $T_{g\ell}$ captures location-specific comparative advantage in good $g$.

Production in each sector is perfectly competitive. Producers choose employment density to maximize profits, taking the prices of goods and input factors as given. In equilibrium, firms make zero profits in each sector and locations with positive production. If a land plot is used for manufacturing or non-tradables, land rents are equal to revenue per unit of land minus labor costs per unit of land at the equilibrium of employment density: 

\begin{equation}
    r_{i\ell} = \max_{n_{i\ell}} \{ P_{i\ell} q_i (n_{i\ell}) - w_\ell n_{i\ell} \}, \quad  i \in  \{M, N\}
\end{equation}

If a land plot is used in agriculture, it is allocated to the agricultural good with the highest rent
\begin{equation}
    r_{\ell j} = \max_{g=1, \ldots, G} \{r_{g\ell j}\}, \quad
    r_{g\ell j} = \max_{n_{g\ell j}} \left( P_{g\ell} q_{g\ell} (n_{g\ell j}) - w_{\ell} n_{g\ell j} \right).
\end{equation}

Hence, the land-allocation decision depends on land rents in the manufacturing and non-tradable sectors ($r_{M\ell}$, $r_{N\ell}$) and expected land rents in agriculture ($r_{A\ell}=E[r_{\ell j}]$), and if a land plot has been allocated to agriculture, the landowner observes the realizations for productivity of each good and decides which good to produce.

Before deriving the equilibrium wage-rental ratio, I first establish an aggregate measure of agricultural revenue productivity ($z_{A\ell}$). Under equation \eqref{eq:frechet_prod}, there exists a sufficient statistic for the disaggregated agricultural goods on aggregate employment and output in agriculture (see Fajgelbaum and Redding, 2022, for the derivation):

\begin{equation}
z_{A\ell}
=
\Gamma\!\left(\frac{\alpha_A\theta-1}{\alpha_A\theta}\right)^{\alpha_A}
\left[
\sum_{g=1}^G T_{g\ell} P_{g\ell}^\theta
\right]^{\frac{1}{\theta}},
\label{eq:zA}
\end{equation}
where $\Gamma(\cdot)$ is the Gamma function and $\alpha_A\theta>1$ is required for the mean of realized productivity to be finite. The same Fréchet structure pins down the allocation of agricultural land across goods: with a continuum of plots, the share of agricultural land in location $\ell$ allocated to good $g$ equals the probability that an individual plot obtains its highest rent from good $g$,
\begin{equation}
l_{g\ell}
=
\frac{T_{g\ell}P_{g\ell}^\theta}
{\sum_{h=1}^G T_{h\ell}P_{h\ell}^\theta},
\label{eq:landshare}
\end{equation}
which is also the weight of good $g$ in the elasticity of $z_{A\ell}$ with respect to prices. Define adjusted agricultural productivity as

\begin{equation}
\tilde z_{A\ell} \equiv \frac{z_{A\ell}}{E_{T\ell}},
\label{eq:zad}
\end{equation}
where $E_{T\ell}$ is the price index for tradables.

\paragraph{C. Wage-Rental Ratio}~\\

\noindent Let the wage-rental ratio in sector $i$ and location $\ell$ be $\omega_{i\ell} \equiv \frac{w_\ell}{r_{i\ell}}$.

Using profit maximization and the zero-profit condition $P_{i\ell}z_{i\ell} \leq w_\ell^{1-\alpha_i} r_{i\ell}^{\alpha_i}$, which holds with equality in active sectors and where the constant terms $\alpha_i^{-\alpha_i}(1-\alpha_i)^{-(1-\alpha_i)}$ are absorbed into productivities, employment density in any active sector can be written in terms of the equilibrium wage-rental ratio by deriving the marginal product of labor and land
\begin{equation}
    n_{i\ell}=\frac{1-\alpha_i}{\alpha_i}\frac{1}{\omega_{i\ell}}
    \label{eq:n}
\end{equation}
and the sectoral wage-rental ratio is
\begin{equation}
    \omega_{i\ell}=(\frac{w_\ell}{P_{i\ell}z_{i\ell}})^\frac{1}{\alpha_i}
    \label{eq:omega}
\end{equation}

For agriculture, the aggregation result in equation~\eqref{eq:zA} implies
that the sector can be treated as a single-good sector with common
revenue productivity $z_{A\ell}$. Thus, employment density ($n_{A\ell}$) and the wage-rental ratio ($\omega_{A\ell}$) take the same form as equations \eqref{eq:n} and \eqref{eq:omega} but using the expected land rent $r_{A\ell}=E[r_{\ell j}]$) and replacing $P_{i\ell}z_{i\ell}$ with aggregate revenue productivity ($z_{A\ell}$) 


\paragraph{D. General Equilibrium Definition} ~\\

\noindent  A general equilibrium consists of expected utility $u^\ast$, a total population $N$, allocations of population density $n_\ell$, land $\{L_{i\ell}\}_{i=A,M,N}$, and employment density $\{n_{i\ell}\}_{i=A,M,N}$, wages $w_\ell$, land rents $r_\ell$, and prices $\{P_{g\ell}\}_{g=1}^{G}$, $P_{M\ell}$, and $P_{N\ell}$ for all
$\ell \in L$, such that:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\roman*.]
    \item Workers maximize utility and choose their location optimally; $n_\ell L_\ell = \left(\frac{u_\ell}{u^*}\right)^\epsilon N$.
    \item Producers maximize profits and land is allocated optimally across sectors: $r_{\ell} = \max\{r_{A\ell}, r_{M\ell}, r_{N\ell}\}$.
    \item The land market clears in each location: $\sum_{i=A, M, N} L_{i\ell} = L_{\ell}$.
    \item The labor market clears in each location: $\sum_{i=A, M,  N} \left(\frac{L_{i\ell}}{L_{\ell}}\right) n_{i\ell} = n_{\ell}$.
    \item The non-tradables market clears in each location: $c_{N\ell} = (L_{N\ell}/L_{\ell}) q_{N} (n_{N\ell})$.
    \item Tradable goods prices are determined by no-arbitrage: for locations within the trade frontier, if agricultural goods are exported and manufacturing goods are imported, $ P_{g\ell} = P_g^\ast / \delta_{g(\ell, \ell^\ast)}$ and $P_{M\ell} = P_M^\ast \delta_{M(\ell, \ell^\ast)}$.
\end{enumerate}


\paragraph{E. Trade Frontier and Complete Specialization}~\\

\noindent I now characterize which locations trade with the world market. To obtain a scalar trade frontier, I assume that the fundamentals relevant for the trade decision are common across locations, $T_{g\ell}=T_g$ for all $g$ and $z_{M\ell}=z_M$, so that all location-level heterogeneity relevant for the open/closed decision is summarized by travel time $t_\ell$. \footnote{If $\{T_{g\ell}\}_{g=1}^G$ or $z_{M\ell}$ vary across locations, the trade decision depends on these fundamentals in addition to $t_\ell$, so the frontier is a surface rather than a scalar cutoff in travel time; the monotonicity argument below continues to apply holding fundamentals fixed.} Using equations \eqref{eq:zA} and \eqref{eq:zad}, aggregate agricultural revenue productivity can then be written as a function of travel time alone:
\begin{equation}
z_A(t)
=
\Gamma\!\left(\frac{\alpha_A\theta-1}{\alpha_A\theta}\right)^{\alpha_A}
\left[
\sum_{g=1}^G T_g (P_g^\ast)^\theta e^{-\theta \tau_g t}
\right]^{1/\theta}.
\label{eq:zA_t}
\end{equation}

Consider a candidate open equilibrium in which location $\ell$ exports agricultural goods and imports manufactures, so that factor prices are pinned down by the agricultural zero-profit condition, $w_\ell^{1-\alpha_A} r_\ell^{\alpha_A}=z_A(t_\ell)$. Producing manufactures locally is unprofitable in this equilibrium if and only if the delivered import price falls short of the local unit cost of manufacturing production:
\begin{equation}
P_M(t_\ell)\, z_M \;\leq\; w_\ell^{1-\alpha_M} r_\ell^{\alpha_M},
\qquad
P_M(t)\equiv P_M^\ast e^{\tau_M t}.
\label{eq:M_margin}
\end{equation}
With a common land intensity in the two traded sectors ($\alpha_M=\alpha_A$), the right-hand side equals $z_A(t_\ell)$, and condition \eqref{eq:M_margin} reduces to $\Omega(t_\ell)\geq 1$, where $\Omega(t)$ is the openness index
\begin{equation}
\Omega(t)
\equiv
\frac{z_A(t)}{P_M(t)\, z_M}
=
\frac{
\Gamma\!\left(\frac{\alpha_A\theta-1}{\alpha_A\theta}\right)^{\alpha_A}
\left[
\sum_{g=1}^G T_g (P_g^\ast)^\theta e^{-\theta \tau_g t}
\right]^{1/\theta}
}{
P_M^\ast e^{\tau_M t} z_M
}.
\label{eq:openness_index}
\end{equation}
The openness index compares the location's revenue productivity in its export sector with the delivered cost of obtaining manufactures through trade. When $\Omega(t_\ell)\geq 1$, importing manufactures dominates producing them locally and the location is open; when $\Omega(t_\ell)<1$, the location produces its own manufactures and stops importing. The threshold value of one is thus derived from the zero-profit conditions rather than imposed. \footnote{I assume throughout that no location finds it profitable to export manufactures, i.e., $P_M^\ast e^{-\tau_M t} z_M < z_A(t)$ on $[0,\bar T]$, consistent with the maintained assumption that all locations have a comparative advantage in agriculture relative to the rest of the world.}

\begin{proposition}[Existence of a trade frontier]
\label{prop:trade_frontier}
Suppose $T_{g\ell}=T_g$ for all $g$, $z_{M\ell}=z_M$, and $\alpha_M=\alpha_A$. If $\Omega(0)>1$ and $\Omega(\bar T)<1$, there exists a unique cutoff $\bar t \in (0,\bar T)$ such that $\Omega(\bar t)=1$; every location with $t_\ell \leq \bar t$ is open, exporting agricultural goods and importing manufactures, and every location with $t_\ell>\bar t$ produces manufactures locally and does not import them.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Continuity follows immediately from \eqref{eq:openness_index}, since $\Omega(t)$ is composed of finite sums, exponentials, products, and powers of positive terms.

To show monotonicity, differentiate $\log \Omega(t)$:
\begin{align}
\frac{d}{dt}\log \Omega(t)
&=
\frac{1}{\theta}
\frac{
\sum_{g=1}^G T_g (P_g^\ast)^\theta (-\theta \tau_g)e^{-\theta \tau_g t}
}{
\sum_{g=1}^G T_g (P_g^\ast)^\theta e^{-\theta \tau_g t}
}
-\tau_M \nonumber \\
&=
-\sum_{g=1}^G l_g(t)\tau_g - \tau_M,
\label{eq:dlogOmega}
\end{align}
where
\begin{equation}
l_g(t)
\equiv
\frac{
T_g (P_g^\ast)^\theta e^{-\theta \tau_g t}
}{
\sum_{h=1}^G T_h (P_h^\ast)^\theta e^{-\theta \tau_h t}
},
\qquad
l_g(t)\geq 0,
\qquad
\sum_{g=1}^G l_g(t)=1,
\label{eq:lg_t}
\end{equation}
is the agricultural land share of good $g$ from equation \eqref{eq:landshare}, evaluated at the no-arbitrage prices $P_{g\ell}=P_g^\ast e^{-\tau_g t}$ and $T_{g\ell}=T_g$. Because $\tau_g,\tau_M>0$, equation \eqref{eq:dlogOmega} implies
\[
\frac{d}{dt}\log \Omega(t)<0,
\]
so $\Omega(t)$ is strictly decreasing.

Since $\Omega(t)$ is continuous on $[0,\bar T]$ with $\Omega(0)>1$ and $\Omega(\bar T)<1$, the Intermediate Value Theorem implies that there exists at least one $\bar t \in (0,\bar T)$ such that $\Omega(\bar t)=1$. Since $\Omega(t)$ is strictly decreasing, $\bar t$ is unique.

Finally, strict monotonicity implies
\[
t\leq \bar t \;\Rightarrow\; \Omega(t)\geq 1,
\qquad
t>\bar t \;\Rightarrow\; \Omega(t)<1.
\]
By condition \eqref{eq:M_margin}, locations with $t_\ell \leq \bar t$ import manufactures and are open, while those with $t_\ell > \bar t$ produce manufactures locally.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
\label{rem:partial_autarky}
Locations beyond the frontier need not be in complete autarky: a location with $t_\ell>\bar t$ may continue to export a subset of agricultural goods with low transport-cost sensitivity, and export margins close good by good as travel time increases, following the ordering of $\tau_g$ (cf. Proposition \ref{prop:specialization}). The frontier $\bar t$ therefore characterizes the manufacturing-import margin rather than complete autarky.
\end{remark}

\begin{corollary}[The frontier moves inland as travel times fall]
\label{cor:frontier_inland}
Suppose an infrastructure improvement lowers travel times pointwise, so that $t'_\ell \leq t_\ell$ for all locations $\ell$, with strict inequality for a non-empty set of locations. Then the set of open locations under $t'_\ell$ weakly contains the set of open locations under $t_\ell$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
By Proposition \ref{prop:trade_frontier}, $\Omega(t)$ is strictly decreasing in travel time. Hence
\[
t'_\ell \leq t_\ell
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\Omega(t'_\ell)\geq \Omega(t_\ell).
\]
Therefore any location that was open before remains open after the infrastructure improvement, and some locations that previously produced manufactures locally may switch to importing them. Thus the frontier moves inland.
\end{proof}



\paragraph{F. Local Equilibrium}~\\

\noindent Consider a location within the trade frontier ($t_\ell\leq\bar t$). By the analysis in Section 3.E, such a location produces no manufactures, so its active sectors are agriculture and non-tradables; the non-tradable sector always operates because the CES preferences in \eqref{eq:utility} imply unbounded marginal utility as consumption of non-tradables approaches zero. Since land is allocated to the sector with the highest rent, both active sectors pay a common land rent $r_\ell$ in equilibrium, so I write $\omega_\ell \equiv w_\ell/r_\ell$ for the location-level wage-rental ratio. Let $\lambda_{A\ell}$ and $\nu_{A\ell}$ denote the agricultural land and labor share respectively.

Under CES demand, tradable expenditure share is given by
\begin{equation}
s_{T\ell}=\beta_T\left(\frac{E_{T\ell}}{E_\ell}\right)^{1-\sigma},
\label{eq:sT_def}
\end{equation}
where $E_\ell$ is the overall consumption price index.

Combining profit maximization in non-tradables from equation \eqref{eq:n} and non-tradable market clearing in each location, we obtain: 
\begin{equation}
    \frac{N_{N\ell}}{L_\ell} = (1-\alpha_N)(1-s_{T\ell})\left(n_\ell+\omega_\ell^{-1}\right)
    \label{eq:Nlabor}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \frac{L_{N\ell}}{L_\ell}= \alpha_N (1-s_{T\ell}) \omega_\ell \left(n_\ell+\omega_\ell^{-1}\right)
\end{equation}

Then land market clearing condition can be re-written as 
\begin{equation}
    \alpha_N(1-s_{T\ell})\left(n_\ell+\frac{1}{\omega_\ell}\right)
+ \frac{\lambda_{A\ell}}{\omega_\ell}= \frac{1}{\omega_\ell}
\label{eq:labor market clearing}
\end{equation}

and combining profit maximization in agriculture from equation \eqref{eq:n}, labor market clearing condition can be re-written as 
\begin{equation}
    (1-\alpha_N)(1-s_{T\ell})\left(n_\ell+\frac{1}{\omega_\ell}\right)
+ \frac{1-\alpha_A}{\alpha_A}\frac{\lambda_{A\ell}}{\omega_\ell}= n_\ell
\end{equation}

Adding up these two equations gives agricultural land share as

\begin{equation}
    \lambda_{A\ell}=\alpha_A s_{T\ell}(1+\omega_\ell n_\ell)
    \label{eq:lambda}
\end{equation}

Plugging this back to equation \eqref{eq:labor market clearing} to solve for population density

\begin{equation}
n_\ell
=
\left(
\frac{1}{\alpha_N + (\alpha_A-\alpha_N)s_{T\ell}} - 1
\right)
\frac{1}{\omega_\ell},
\label{eq:density}
\end{equation}

By definition, the agricultural labor share can be written as $\nu_{A\ell}=\frac{N_{A\ell}/L_\ell}{n_\ell}$. Using equations \eqref{eq:Nlabor}, \eqref{eq:lambda} and \eqref{eq:density}, 

\begin{equation}
\nu_{A\ell}
=
\frac{(1-\alpha_A)s_{T\ell}}
{1-\alpha_N-(\alpha_A-\alpha_N)s_{T\ell}}.
\label{eq:nuA}
\end{equation}


To derive the sufficient statistics for local equilibrium, using zero-profit conditions in the two active sectors, factor prices satisfy
\begin{align}
w_\ell &= z_{A\ell}\,\omega_\ell^{\alpha_A},
\label{eq:wage}\\
r_\ell &= z_{A\ell}\,\omega_\ell^{\alpha_A-1},
\label{eq:rent}\\
P_{N\ell} &= \frac{z_{A\ell}}{z_{N\ell}}\,\omega_\ell^{\alpha_A-\alpha_N}.
\label{eq:PN}
\end{align}

Expressing the common component of utility in equation \eqref{eq:utility} in the indirect utility form $u_\ell=w_\ell/E_\ell$ together with equation \eqref{eq:wage} gives
\begin{align}
\frac{E_{T\ell}}{E_\ell}
&=
\frac{u_\ell}{\omega_\ell^{\alpha_A}\tilde z_{A\ell}},
\label{eq:ETE}\\
\frac{P_{N\ell}}{E_\ell}
&=
\frac{u_\ell}{\omega_\ell^{\alpha_N}z_{N\ell}},
\label{eq:PNE}
\end{align}
and therefore
\begin{equation}
s_{T\ell}
=
\beta_T
\left(
\frac{u_\ell}{\tilde z_{A\ell} \omega_\ell^{\alpha_A}}
\right)^{1-\sigma}.
\label{eq:sT}
\end{equation}

Substituting these expressions into the CES price-index identity yields
\begin{equation}
u_\ell
=
\left[
\beta_T \left(\tilde z_{A\ell}\,\omega_\ell^{\alpha_A}\right)^{\sigma-1}
+
\left(1-\beta_T\right)\left(z_{N\ell}\,\omega_\ell^{\alpha_N}\right)^{\sigma-1}
\right]^{\frac{1}{\sigma-1}},
\label{eq:omega_eq}
\end{equation}
Given $u_\ell$, equation \eqref{eq:omega_eq} determines a unique wage-rental ratio $\omega_\ell$: with $\sigma<1$, its right-hand side is strictly increasing in $\omega_\ell$ and spans $(0,\infty)$. The local equilibrium is then closed as follows. Equations \eqref{eq:omega_eq} and \eqref{eq:density} jointly define a downward-sloping local labor demand relationship between $u_\ell$ and $n_\ell$ (given $\sigma<1$ and $\alpha_A>\alpha_N$), while the Fréchet location choice in equation \eqref{eq:density_supply} defines an upward-sloping local labor supply; their intersection determines $(u_\ell, \omega_\ell, n_\ell)$ given the economy-wide expected utility $u^\ast$. At the national level, $u^\ast$ adjusts so that location populations sum to $N$. Existence and uniqueness of the general equilibrium follow from the same argument as in Fajgelbaum and Redding (2022, Proposition 1).

Thus, conditional on the economy-wide expected utility $u^\ast$, adjusted agricultural productivity ($\tilde{z}_{A\ell}$) and non-tradable productivity ($z_{N\ell}$) are sufficient statistics for local equilibrium outcomes
\begin{equation}
n(\ell)=n\!\left(\tilde z_A(\ell),z_N(\ell)\right),
\qquad
\nu_A(\ell)=\nu_A\!\left(\tilde z_A(\ell),z_N(\ell)\right).
\label{eq:reduced_local}
\end{equation}


I first establish that better market access raises adjusted agricultural productivity, which is the first link in the chain from trade costs to structural transformation.

\begin{lemma}[Trade costs and adjusted agricultural productivity]
\label{lem:zad}
Suppose location $\ell$ is open, exports every agricultural good ($l_{g\ell} > \gamma_g$ for all $g$), and imports manufactures. Then
\begin{equation}
\frac{d \ln \tilde z_{A\ell}}{d t_\ell}
= -\Big[\sum_{g=1}^G \left(l_{g\ell}-\gamma_g\right)\tau_g + (1-\gamma_A)\tau_M\Big] < 0,
\label{eq:zad_t}
\end{equation}
so a reduction in travel time strictly raises adjusted agricultural productivity.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Log-differentiating equation \eqref{eq:zA} with respect to $t_\ell$ using the no-arbitrage prices $P_{g\ell}=P_g^\ast e^{-\tau_g t_\ell}$ gives $d\ln z_{A\ell}/dt_\ell = -\sum_g l_{g\ell}\tau_g$, where the weights are the land shares in equation \eqref{eq:landshare}. Log-differentiating the tradables price index \eqref{eq:ET} with $P_{M\ell}=P_M^\ast e^{\tau_M t_\ell}$ gives $d\ln E_{T\ell}/dt_\ell = -\sum_g \gamma_g\tau_g + (1-\gamma_A)\tau_M$. Subtracting the second expression from the first and using $\tilde z_{A\ell}=z_{A\ell}/E_{T\ell}$ yields \eqref{eq:zad_t}, which is strictly negative since $l_{g\ell}>\gamma_g$ and $\tau_g,\tau_M>0$.
\end{proof}

The exporting condition $l_{g\ell}>\gamma_g$ is not innocuous: better market access raises not only the prices received by local producers but also the local cost of consuming agricultural goods, and the net effect on $\tilde z_{A\ell}$ is positive precisely when the location produces more of each good than it consumes. The condition is always feasible, since land shares sum to one while $\sum_g \gamma_g=\gamma_A<1$.

\begin{proposition}[Spatial Balassa-Samuelson effect]
\label{prop:bs}
Assume agricultural and non-tradable goods are complements ($\sigma<1$), agriculture is more land-intensive than non-tradables ($\alpha_A>\alpha_N$), and labor is perfectly mobile ($\epsilon\to\infty$), and consider a location small enough that $u^\ast$ is unaffected by its outcomes. Then an increase in adjusted agricultural productivity $\tilde z_{A\ell}$, holding $z_{N\ell}$ fixed, (i) lowers the wage-rental ratio $\omega_\ell$; (ii) lowers the tradable expenditure share $s_{T\ell}$; (iii) raises population density $n_\ell$; and (iv) lowers the agricultural employment share $\nu_{A\ell}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
With perfect mobility, $u_\ell = u^\ast$ for all populated locations. Define $x_\ell \equiv \tilde z_{A\ell}\,\omega_\ell^{\alpha_A}$ and $y_\ell \equiv z_{N\ell}\,\omega_\ell^{\alpha_N}$, so that equation \eqref{eq:omega_eq} reads
\[
\beta_T\, x_\ell^{\sigma-1} + (1-\beta_T)\, y_\ell^{\sigma-1} = (u^\ast)^{\sigma-1}.
\]
(i) Since $\sigma<1$, the left-hand side is strictly decreasing in both $\omega_\ell$ and $\tilde z_{A\ell}$; holding $u^\ast$ fixed, an increase in $\tilde z_{A\ell}$ therefore requires $\omega_\ell$ to fall. (ii) A lower $\omega_\ell$ with $z_{N\ell}$ fixed lowers $y_\ell$ and hence raises $y_\ell^{\sigma-1}$; the identity then requires $x_\ell^{\sigma-1}$ to fall, i.e., $x_\ell$ to rise. By equation \eqref{eq:sT}, $s_{T\ell}=\beta_T (u^\ast/x_\ell)^{1-\sigma}$ falls. (iii) In equation \eqref{eq:density}, the term in parentheses is positive and strictly decreasing in $s_{T\ell}$ given $\alpha_A>\alpha_N$, so a lower $s_{T\ell}$ raises it; combined with a lower $\omega_\ell$, population density $n_\ell$ rises. (iv) In equation \eqref{eq:nuA}, the numerator is increasing and the denominator decreasing in $s_{T\ell}$, so $\nu_{A\ell}$ is strictly increasing in $s_{T\ell}$ and therefore falls.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Effects of travel-time reductions]
\label{cor:tt}
Under the assumptions of Lemma \ref{lem:zad} and Proposition \ref{prop:bs}, a reduction in travel time $t_\ell$ raises population density $n_\ell$, the non-tradable (urban) employment share $1-\nu_{A\ell}$, and GDP per unit of land, defined as $Y_\ell \equiv w_\ell n_\ell + r_\ell$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
By Lemma \ref{lem:zad}, a lower $t_\ell$ raises $\tilde z_{A\ell}$, so Proposition \ref{prop:bs} delivers the results for $n_\ell$ and $1-\nu_{A\ell}$. For GDP, write $Y_\ell = r_\ell (1+\omega_\ell n_\ell)$. From equation \eqref{eq:rent}, $r_\ell = z_{A\ell}\,\omega_\ell^{\alpha_A-1}$: a lower $t_\ell$ raises $z_{A\ell}$ (Lemma \ref{lem:zad}) and lowers $\omega_\ell$ (Proposition \ref{prop:bs}), and since $\alpha_A-1<0$ both movements raise $r_\ell$. From equation \eqref{eq:density}, $\omega_\ell n_\ell = \left[\alpha_N+(\alpha_A-\alpha_N)s_{T\ell}\right]^{-1}-1$ rises as $s_{T\ell}$ falls. Hence $Y_\ell$ rises.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
\label{rem:mobility}
Perfect mobility is stronger than necessary. Fajgelbaum and Redding (2022, Proposition 3) establish results (ii)-(iv) for any finite migration elasticity $\epsilon>0$; only the decline in the wage-rental ratio requires $\epsilon$ above a threshold, and under fully immobile labor $\omega_\ell$ instead rises. The density and employment-share predictions are therefore robust to limited migration, while the direction of the factor-price response depends on the strength of labor mobility.
\end{remark}

The economic intuition is as follows. Better access to world markets raises profitability in the tradable rural sector, which increases income and attracts population. When population rises, the reward to immobile land increases relative to the reward to mobile labor. This induces either more labor-intensive techniques within sectors or a reallocation of labor toward the labor-intensive non-tradable sector. Hence, reductions in internal trade costs to world markets generate structural transformation from tradable rural activity toward non-tradable urban activity, given the inelastic demand between sectors.

\begin{proposition}[Specialization within agriculture]
\label{prop:specialization}
Locations with shorter travel time to the international gateway allocate relatively more land to transport-cost-sensitive agricultural goods: for every good $g$,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d \ln l_{g\ell}}{d t_\ell}
= -\theta \left(\tau_g - \bar{\tau}_\ell\right),
\qquad
\bar{\tau}_\ell \equiv \sum_{h=1}^G l_{h\ell} \tau_h,
\label{eq:landshare_d}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{\tau}_\ell$ is the land-share-weighted average transport-cost sensitivity in location $\ell$. As travel time falls, the land share rises for goods with above-average transport-cost sensitivity ($\tau_g > \bar{\tau}_\ell$) and falls for goods with below-average sensitivity.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Substituting the no-arbitrage prices $P_{g\ell}=P_g^\ast/\delta_{g(\ell,\ell^*)}=P_g^\ast e^{-\tau_g t_\ell}$ into the land shares \eqref{eq:landshare}, define the good-specific competitiveness term
\[
A_{g\ell}(t_\ell)\equiv T_{g\ell}P_{g\ell}(t_\ell)^\theta
= T_{g\ell}(P_g^*)^\theta e^{-\theta\tau_g t_\ell},
\qquad
l_{g\ell}=\frac{A_{g\ell}}{\sum_h A_{h\ell}}.
\]
Then $d\ln A_{g\ell}/dt_\ell = -\theta\tau_g$ and $d \ln \left(\sum_h A_{h\ell}\right)/dt_\ell = -\theta\sum_h l_{h\ell}\tau_h = -\theta\bar{\tau}_\ell$; subtracting the second expression from the first yields \eqref{eq:landshare_d}.
\end{proof}

Intuitively, transport-cost-insensitive goods face little penalty from being produced inland, whereas transport-cost-sensitive goods benefit disproportionately from improved access to ports. Therefore, locations with better export access will specialize in transport-cost-sensitive goods: for the same reduction in $t_\ell$, land shares grow faster for more transport-cost-sensitive goods.



\paragraph{G. Trade Liberalization and the Border Wedge}~\\

\noindent The analysis so far takes the trade regime as given. I now consider changes in the trade regime alongside changes in internal transport costs. To that end, I introduce a border wedge $\chi \in (0,1]$ that summarizes the trade regime: producers of agricultural goods receive only a fraction $\chi$ of the world price at the gateway, and importers of manufactures pay a markup $1/\chi$ over the world price. \footnote{The symmetric treatment of the export and import wedges follows Lerner symmetry: an import tariff is equivalent to an export tax, so protection of the import-competing sector acts as an implicit tax on the export sector. A good-specific wedge $\chi_g$ is a straightforward extension; I impose a common $\chi$ for simplicity.} The no-arbitrage prices in an open location become
\begin{equation}
P_{g\ell} = \chi\, P_g^\ast e^{-\tau_g t_\ell},
\qquad
P_{M\ell} = \frac{P_M^\ast}{\chi}\, e^{\tau_M t_\ell},
\label{eq:prices_chi}
\end{equation}
with $\chi=1$ corresponding to free trade, so that trade liberalization is an increase in $\chi$.

Since aggregate agricultural revenue productivity in equation \eqref{eq:zA} is homogeneous of degree one in prices, and the land shares in equation \eqref{eq:landshare} are homogeneous of degree zero, the openness index scales as
\begin{equation}
\Omega(t;\chi) = \chi^2\, \Omega(t),
\label{eq:omega_chi}
\end{equation}
and the trade frontier $\bar t(\chi)$ solves $\chi^2\, \Omega(\bar t)=1$: a more liberal trade regime supports trade at longer travel times.

\begin{proposition}[Complementarity between internal and external integration]
\label{prop:complementarity}
Suppose the conditions of Proposition \ref{prop:trade_frontier} hold at each trade regime considered, so that the frontier $\bar t(\chi)$ is interior. Then:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item Liberalization moves the frontier inland:
\[
\frac{d \bar t}{d \ln \chi}
= \frac{2}{\sum_{g=1}^G l_g(\bar t)\tau_g + \tau_M} > 0.
\]
\item On the intensive margin, roads and liberalization are separable: for an open location, $\ln \tilde z_{A\ell}$ is additively separable in $\ln\chi$ and $t_\ell$, so the elasticity of adjusted agricultural productivity (and hence of population density and urbanization through Proposition \ref{prop:bs}) with respect to travel time does not depend on the trade regime.
\item On the extensive margin, roads and liberalization are complements: consider a road that lowers travel time by $\Delta>0$ and a liberalization from $\chi$ to $\chi'>\chi$. A location with $\bar t(\chi) < t_\ell - \Delta \leq \bar t(\chi') < t_\ell$ is opened to trade neither by the road alone nor by the liberalization alone, but only by the combination of the two.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(i) Differentiating the frontier condition $2\ln\chi + \ln\Omega(\bar t(\chi))=0$ with respect to $\ln\chi$ and using $d\ln\Omega/dt = -\left[\sum_g l_g(t)\tau_g + \tau_M\right]$ from equation \eqref{eq:dlogOmega} yields the expression, which is strictly positive since $\tau_g,\tau_M>0$.
(ii) From equation \eqref{eq:prices_chi}, $\ln P_{g\ell} = \ln\chi + \ln P_g^\ast - \tau_g t_\ell$, so all prices are log-additive in $(\ln\chi, t_\ell)$, and the land shares $l_{g\ell}$ are invariant to the common scaling of agricultural prices by $\chi$. Hence $\ln z_{A\ell}$ shifts by $\ln\chi$, $\ln E_{T\ell}$ shifts by $(2\gamma_A-1)\ln\chi$, and $\ln \tilde z_{A\ell}$ shifts by $2(1-\gamma_A)\ln\chi>0$, uniformly across locations, while the travel-time derivative in Lemma \ref{lem:zad} is unchanged. Therefore $\partial^2 \ln\tilde z_{A\ell}/\partial\ln\chi\,\partial t_\ell = 0$.
(iii) By Proposition \ref{prop:trade_frontier}, the set of open locations under regime $\chi$ is $\{\ell : t_\ell \leq \bar t(\chi)\}$. With the road alone, the location's travel time $t_\ell-\Delta$ exceeds $\bar t(\chi)$, so it remains closed; with the liberalization alone, its travel time $t_\ell$ exceeds $\bar t(\chi')$, so it remains closed; with both, $t_\ell-\Delta \leq \bar t(\chi')$ and the location opens.
\end{proof}

Proposition \ref{prop:complementarity} implies that the response of a location to a given reduction in internal trade costs depends on the trade regime only through the extensive margin. Under a restrictive regime, a reduction in travel time leaves locations beyond the frontier closed to trade and therefore has no effect on their tradable prices or productivity; once the regime liberalizes, the frontier moves inland and previously accumulated reductions in travel time determine which locations cross it. Part (ii) further shows that liberalization raises adjusted agricultural productivity uniformly across open locations, so cross-sectional differences in outcomes among open locations continue to be driven by differences in travel time.

\vspace{0.5cm}
\noindent This model aims to provide a microeconomic rationale for the reduced-form evidence on
urbanization and agricultural specialization. As locations experience greater reductions in trade costs in accessing world markets, the model rationalizes their higher increase in income, population densities, urban population shares, and a sorting pattern to specialize in goods with comparative advantage. In addition, the trade frontier implies that these effects switch on only once a location becomes integrated with world markets, so that the timing of the response to internal integration depends on the openness of the trade regime.


\section{Data}
For the empirical analysis, I combined information from different sources to construct a dataset on population, urban population, and employment share; disaggregated agricultural commodity production and trade; and travel time to the closest international ports for 532 consistent Brazilian micro-regions in survey years between 1970 and 2010.

\textbf{Population Data.} I collect measures of population at the municipality level from the Demographic Census conducted by the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics (IBGE). The Demographic Census is the main source of reference for understanding the living conditions of the population in all Brazilian municipalities and in their internal territorial divisions. The periodicity of the survey is decennial, except for 1990, whose operation was postponed to 1991. I further collect measures separately for urban and rural populations to account for the evolution of urbanization.

\textbf{Employment Data.} Due to unavailability of data on jobs in the formal sector, I collect information on the economically active population at the municipality level from the Institute of Applied Economic Research. This measure accounts for the number of people who are employed or looking for work, separately for rural and urban areas.

\textbf{Agricultural Production Data.} To measure agricultural production, I use Municipal Agricultural Production (PAM) conducted by IBGE. The survey provides detailed information on planted area and production for 64 agricultural products. The collection at the municipality level only started after 1973, so I collect production for the years 1973, 1980, 1991, 2000, and 2010 to be consistent with population and employment data.

For all GIS data, I use the SIRGAS 2000 (EPSG:4674) coordinate system. To compute distances, I project the shapefile using the Brazil SIRGAS 2000 Polyconic (EPSG:5880) projection.

\textbf{Geographical Unit Data.} The analysis in the paper occurs at either the micro-region or the meso-region level. \footnote{Data are aggregated to meso-region level for robustness.} Municipality boundaries change over time: to generate consistent boundaries from 1970, I construct a set of 3800 consistent minimum comparable regions following Ehrl (2017) based on the official division of the Brazilian territory produced by IBGE and further aggregate them to micro-region and meso-region.

\textbf{Road Data.} The main data source for roads between 1960 and 2010 is the Brazilian Ministry of Transportation, with shapefiles available for each ten-year period between 1960 and 2010. \footnote{Since the link to access the database appeared to be broken since March 2024, I used the replication data from Morten and Oliveria (2024), which was downloaded from the above source on 3/21/2021, and the shapefiles are appended together to generate the cumulative road network for 1970, 1980, 1990, 2000, and 2010.} The attribute table contains a variable that indicates the road surface status (planned, paved, or unpaved). Following Morten and Oliveria (2024), my analysis sample is all roads that are either paved or unpaved. \footnote{I drop leg\_multim that contains “Planejada”}

\textbf{Port Data.} I collect the shapefile of all ports in Brazil from the Brazilian Ministry of Transportation. Ports that export agricultural commodities were identified based on Agrostat, a database for Brazilian agricultural exports, which records the ports involved in the transaction. Among them, I select 12 ports based on Victoria et al. (2021), excluding ports with very low agricultural exports or not in operation before 1970.


\section{Identification Strategy}

In this section, I first consider long-differenced estimation that compares the change in regions' economic outcomes from 1970 to 2010. I then turn to a dynamic estimation that considers decadal data throughout this 40-year period.

\subsection{Instrument Construction}

The main concern of the identification is that the exact location of the highways may have been chosen based on economic attributes and targeted regions that would have experienced different growth trajectories if not connected. On the one hand, many highways are concessioned to private sectors, whose search for profits could have led them to select regions that otherwise would have grown more rapidly. On the other hand, the Brazilian government may place highways to promote rural area development that would have otherwise grown less rapidly. To address these concerns about nonrandom placement, I construct an instrument based on the project lines in the documentation of the Plan for National Integration in 1973 for highway construction.

I create a time-invariant treatment intensity measure for each micro-region by modeling the network using GIS and then employing the fast marching algorithm. \footnote{Allen and Arkolakis (2014) first undertook the algorithm to calculate travel times, and it has since then been used in Alder (2016), Pellegrina and Sotelo (2021), and Morten and Oliveira (2024). Other trade papers (e.g., Baun-Snow (2008) and Donaldson (2012)) have computed the travel time using the Dijkstra algorithm on a discrete graph. This algorithm, however, is not appropriate for approximating distance on a continuous surface in this paper.} This algorithm solves the Eikonal equation, which characterizes the propagation of wave fronts and calculates the most efficient travel route between points on a grid. I construct a grid of Brazil where each cell's value represents the travel speed through that cell. Following Pellegrina and Sotelo (2021), I assume travel speeds of 60 km/h on paved roads, 30 km/h on unpaved roads, and 15 km/h through areas without roads. I run the fast marching algorithm to calculate travel times on the PIN road network from the centroid of each micro-region to the nearest international port and repeat the process to calculate travel times on the actual 1970 network, which creates a comparable no-PIN travel time. I then calculate the log change in predicted travel time by subtracting the log PIN travel time from the 1970 travel time. \footnote{Note that this log change in predicted travel time is in absolute value: the greater the reduction, the more positive it gets.} The resulting variable, which I label the PIN-induced reduction in travel time, approximates the predicted percent change in travel time spurred by the integration.

The validity of using the PIN plan as an instrument depends on the fact that the highway network of the PIN construction plan, which are the 1973 counterparts of the 2010 highway network, was not meant to move agricultural goods. Moreover, it was organized to fulfill the historical objective to strengthen national defense and security back in the military dictatorship. The identifying assumption here is that the reductions in travel time induced by PIN affect changes in microregion-level economic outcomes only through the reductions in travel time induced by the actual road expansion, i.e., the instrument \(\text{PIN-induced Reduction in TT}_{is}\) is uncorrelated with unobservables in \(\epsilon_{is}\) conditional on controls \(X_i\) and \(\theta_s\). Therefore, I expect the 1973 highway construction plan to indicate routes where roads can be constructed at low cost but will not reflect modern travel and shipping demand.

\subsection{Long-Differenced Estimation}

Long-differenced estimation compares regions' economic outcomes in 1970, the year prior to the start of the PIN construction, with regions' economic outcomes in 2010.

The baseline specification for the long-differences estimation is the following:

\begin{equation}
\Delta logY_{is} = \alpha+\beta \Delta log \text{Travel Time}_{is} + log(Y_{is, 1970}) + \eta X_{is} + \theta_s + \epsilon_{is}
\end{equation}

where the outcome variables $Y_i$ are expressed in logs. My micro-region sample is constructed such that the outcomes are continuously observed, but I do not apply this requirement for disaggregated agricultural commodities and use $log(Y_{is}+1)$ in estimation for microregions with 0 production in 1970 in order to model the extensive margin and maintain a consistent sample. $X_{is}$ controls for the micro-region's geographical characteristics. \(\theta_s\) is the state-fixed effect, and errors are clustered at the state level. 

The long-differenced approach is transparent and allows us to control easily for long-run trends during the 1970-2010 period. All estimations include the initial level of the outcome variable $Y_{i, 1970}$ in the micro-region to adjust for heterogeneity in starting points and capture flexibly issues related to economic convergence across regions. To control for the fact that some micro-regions within Brazil could have higher or lower growth than others for reasons unrelated to the highway network (e.g., agro-climatic conditions and landscape), I follow Fajgelbaum and Redding (2022) to control separately for latitude and longitude. Finally, to address the concern that the exclusion restriction could be violated as a larger region has a higher probability of lying along a highway construction plan, I control for log land area. Conditional on these controls, my IV estimation assumes that there is no direct effect on economic activity of lying along the PIN construction network, other than through the probability of a highway connection. 

\subsection{Dynamic Estimation}

As the long-difference method compares changes over a long period between two points in time, typically before and after an intervention or treatment, it essentially collapses all the changes over the period into a single estimate and can overlook important dynamics that might explain the overall effect. Thus, I further examine whether the main outcomes of interest—actual road travel times, population, urban population share, and agricultural production—responded to the PIN-induced reduction in travel times, and if so, when did the effect kick in. To do this, I estimate the equation

\begin{equation}
logY_{it} = \alpha + \sum_{k \neq \text{1970}} \beta_{k}\text{PIN-induced Reduction in TT}_{i} \times I(t=k) + \mu_{i} + \lambda_{t} + v_{it}
\end{equation}

where the dependent variable is either GDP, population, urban population share, or agricultural production of micro-region $i$ in year $t$; $\mu_i$ is the micro-region fixed effects; $\lambda_t$ is the year fixed effect; and $v_{it}$ is the idiosyncratic error term cluster at the micro-region level. I normalize the coefficient on year $1970$ (the last observation pre-PIN) to equal zero.

While road transport by trucks is the most widely used method of transport in Brazil, waterways continue to play an important role in many regions where agricultural commodities would be loaded into barges and travel through rivers to the exporting ports. For regions endowed with natural waterway access, their percentage decrease of the actual trade cost may not be as large as the transport cost reduction manifested by road construction, i.e., they are less dependent on road transport because they are able to substitute roads with their pre-existing transport mode of waterway.

\subsubsection{Core and Periphery}

The reduction of transport costs could benefit core or peripheral regions unequally. Regions that gain more from better world market access will be the regions that rely more heavily on foreign demand. If a region is close to a state capital, it is more likely to trade more frequently with the state capital and have stronger domestic demand. The access to a foreign market thus matters less for these regions.

In particular, while both core and peripheral micro-regions are estimated to have experienced positive GDP and urbanization growth with greater trade cost reductions, peripheral micro-regions are estimated to have experienced marginally positive growth effects. 





\section{Robustness Checks}


\subsection{Alternative Robust Estimator}
Although satisfying the parallel trend assumption in the event study specification alleviates the endogeneity concern that my estimates are biased, it cannot guarantee that the results are causal. I present additional evidence on the robustness of my instrument to support the causal claim of the main results.

Recent literature has indicated that the conventional practice of implementing event studies via two-way fixed effect regressions may not adequately handle heterogeneity in treatment effects (de Chaisemartin and D’Haultfœuille, 2020; Sun and Abraham, 2021; Callaway and Sant’Anna, 2021; Goodman-Bacon, 2021; Borusyak et al., 2024). The assumption of homogeneous treatment effects is often implicitly embedded in static DiD regressions, potentially resulting in estimands that assign negative weights to some long-term treatment effects. In cases of dynamic estimation, the model leverages comparisons between newly treated groups and those treated earlier, which are only valid under the homogeneity assumption. The heterogeneity in treatment can significantly distort the estimator’s weights on treatment effects and therefore bias the result. Therefore, it's fundamental to allow and explicitly model the treatment heterogeneity. I used two robust estimators from Borusyak et al. (2024) and Callaway and Sant'Anna (2021) to circumvent the issue of mismatch between treatment-effect heterogeneity and specifications that restrict it. These DID estimators require a discrete treatment, so I define a micro-region as treated if the reduction of its travel time on the PIN network is above the median.

\subsection{Pseudo Poisson Maximum Likelihood}

In the main analysis, I estimate an average treatment effect in logs of the form $E_P[log(Y_1)-log(Y_0)]$ to approximate percentage changes in the outcomes. While zeros are not present in the GDP and population outcomes, they could pose a problem for estimating the growth of agricultural production since the agricultural production data is sparser, so ATE in logs is not well-defined. I employ alternative transformations using $log(Y_i+1)$ to handle zeros, which behaves similarly to $log(Y)$ but is well-defined at zero. However, this method may introduce biases as the treatment effect estimates are sensitive to the choice of the scale constant, particularly when the extensive margin effect is large. (Chen and Roth, 2024). 

I estimate the elasticity of agricultural production to travel time by Pseudo Poisson Maximum Likelihood (PPML) to inherently accommodate zero flows in the dependent variable. PPML allows for the inclusion of zero production values without necessitating any transformation. Estimating in levels is particularly advantageous for interpreting elasticities in a straightforward manner. The following PPML specification is used to mimic the long-differenced regression in logs:

\begin{equation}
    E(Y_{is, 2010} \mid \boldsymbol{X}) = \exp\left(\alpha + \log(Y_{is, 1970}) + \beta \Delta \log \text{Travel Time}_{is} + \eta X_{is} + \theta_s + \epsilon_{is}\right)
\end{equation}

where the coefficient of the initial level of the dependent variable is constrained to 1. \footnote{Due to the attempt to mimic the long-differenced regression, I still need to perform the transformation on the left-hand side initial level of the outcome, but since zeros are also prevalent on the right-hand side, I still expect PPML to alleviate the bias of equation (1).}

\section{Discussion}

In this section, I synthesize my main results and discuss potential concerns of this paper.

\subsection{Link to Theoretical Framework}

The study examines the structural transformation and specialization within the tradable sector after a transport cost shock. Four key results were identified. First, for micro-regions with a larger reduction in transport costs, there is a larger growth in GDP, population, urban population, and urban population shares. The effects are persistent and getting stronger within the 40-year period after the road construction. Second, micro-regions with larger reductions in transport costs see a larger growth in soybean production, followed by a medium-sized growth in cotton and minor growth in cattle. Third, this increase in soybean production is driven by both extensive and intensive margins, while the increase in cotton is only driven by a growth within the intensive margin. Last, the effect of transport cost reductions marks heterogeneity across Brazil, as such an effect will be more prominent in micro-regions where the newly built roads will be particularly valuable. 

To better understand these results, I emphasize the importance of linking them back to the theoretical model. There are two main hypotheses the model predicts: 1) Locations with greater reductions in trade costs to world markets will have a larger increase in GDP, population, and urban population shares. 2) Locations with better market access will specialize in transport-cost-sensitive agricultural goods. 

Overall, the above results depict a pattern that is largely consistent with the model predictions. Micro-regions see a structural transformation from a productivity increase in the tradable rural sector to the growth in the non-tradable urban sector, explained by the spatial Balassa-Samuelson effect. Micro-regions that engage in trade activities increase their income, which translates into a higher demand for both tradable and non-tradable goods. Since non-tradable goods can only be provided by local labor, the increase in demand pushes wages up in the micro-region, which brings migrants from other regions of Brazil to work in the non-tradable (urban) sector. This in-migration then reduces wages in the micro-region, pushing the local economy back to the equilibrium. Moreover, micro-regions with greater reductions in transport costs indeed specialize in more transport-cost-sensitive agricultural goods. Soybean has the largest growth in production, while the growth in the transport-cost-insensitive cattle is negligible. The significant growth shown in the production growth of cotton, another transport-cost-insensitive good as in Table 1, marks an exception. However, this can be explained by the variation in agricultural productivity across land plots for different agricultural products in the model's set-up. Landowners allocate land shares based on a combination of productivity, price, and wage. The transport cost reduction together with the technological advancement in soybean cultivation drives landowners to exploit the new comparative advantage of producing soybeans at the extensive margin, leaving more land plots in the intensive margin to cotton to grow. Furthermore, although the model predicts a certain percentage of reduction in trade costs should affect the micro-regions across Brazil equally, the heterogeneity observed across Brazil can also be rationalized in the model. The main theme of all the heterogeneity we observe is that the effect is stronger in micro-regions where the new infrastructure is more valuable. All three heterogeneous effects can be integrated into this theme, as micro-regions endowed with pre-existing waterway access, in regions better served with infrastructure, and having more domestic trade opportunities are all less dependent on the transport cost reductions brought by new roads. Therefore, the percentage reduction in transport costs may not be fully manifested in the actual reduction in trade costs.

Finally, the timing of the effects maps directly into the trade-frontier mechanism of Proposition \ref{prop:complementarity}. Roads built during the 1970s reduced travel times for interior micro-regions, but under the restrictive pre-1990 trade regime many of these locations remained beyond the trade frontier, so the model predicts muted responses to the road network in the first two decades. The liberalization of the early 1990s, which lowered average nominal tariffs from 54.9\% in 1987 to 10.5\% in 1994, corresponds to an increase in the border wedge $\chi$: the frontier shifted inland and the pre-existing road network was activated for the locations that then crossed it. This rationalizes why the event-study estimates in Section 6.2 show effects materializing disproportionately after 1990, and why the extensive-margin entry into transport-cost-sensitive soybeans documented in Section 6.3 is concentrated in the newly integrated Center-West. The model also delivers a testable restriction: the treatment effect of road access should interact positively with the post-liberalization period at the extensive margin, while intensive-margin elasticities should remain stable across trade regimes.

\subsection{Concerns}

I also highlight and discuss the following main concerns of this paper.

\textbf{Measurement Error.} A potential concern is the presence of measurement error in the treatment. The travel time to the closest international port is computed using the Fast Marching algorithm. This algorithm, while being effective and widely used for numerous applications, tends to incorrectly assign large values to centroids that are in close proximity to the port (starting seeds). To address this issue, I assign the minimum value of travel time to all of these centroids. However, such manual correction could further put additional noise into the measurement of the actual change in transport cost, introducing an attenuation bias in the estimated effects. Furthermore, since my instrument PIN-induced reduction in travel time is constructed the same way as the treatment, it cannot be used to resolve such attenuation bias. This could result in the coefficients being biased towards zero, potentially underestimating the true effect of the treatment and explaining the relatively small magnitude in some of the outcomes. 

\textbf{Alternative Channel.} There are concerns that my main results could be explained by other channels other than integration into the world market happening in my sample period. An immediate possibility is the internal integration, i.e., the internal trade between regions, as opposed to the external integration. Since the Plan for National Integration is planned to integrate the West into the National Economy, the failure to account for domestic trade could be a main concern. The estimated effect size could capture both the effect from an increase in both domestic and international trade, as those regions with a larger reduction in transport cost to ports are likely to coincide with those that experience a larger reduction in transport cost to nearby regions. To more precisely decompose the effect from internal and external integration, I could regress the outcome on measures of access to both the domestic market and the world market. With time permits, several measures of domestic market access can be employed, such as the length of the road network within a certain radius or the market potential. The former is derived using information about roads, the same as the travel time to international ports, while the latter one is a traditional gravity measure equal to the discounted sum of GDP surrounding each micro-region. Despite this feasibility to separate the effect of internal trade, the event study in section 6.2 should alleviate concern that the estimated effect size is biased upward due to domestic trade, since the significant effect hadn't kicked in until the trade liberalization.

\textbf{Insufficient Response.} While the positive estimates in Table 2 support the model’s prediction of rising income and urbanization, only GDP and urban population growth are statistically significant; population growth estimates remain insignificant across all panels. Tracing back to the model, this potentially suggests a smaller than expected labor mobility parameter $\epsilon$. 

This limited population mobility could help explain the muted response in urban population share: although trade cost reductions attract tradable sector activity and raise income, the resulting demand for non-tradables (which rely on local labor) draws some labor into urban sectors. However, if migration is limited, this pressure does not fully raise land rents, and a portion of labor remains in the rural tradable sector.

Low labor mobility may also explain why peripheral regions benefit more than core areas—contrary to standard agglomeration models. The common foundation of the core-periphery effect of trade integration is that while falling trade costs reduce the strength of both agglomeration forces and dispersion forces, they tend to attenuate the agglomeration forces at a lower rate compared to the dispersion forces. \footnote{Equilibrium profits are a positive function of access to consumer expenditure and decreasing in access to competing industrial producers. On one hand, lower trade costs decrease the relative disadvantage of higher product market competition in the larger market because the relative increase in competition is stronger for the smaller region. On the other hand, lower trade costs also decrease the market access advantage of the larger region because the relative increase in market access is stronger for the smaller region.} Previous literature like Faber (2014) and Baum-Snow (2020) showed that falling trade costs attenuate the locational advantage of producing in a remote location at a faster rate than the agglomeration advantage of producing in the core region, so a given reduction in trade costs induces more concentration in the core region. While Krugman (1991) predicted that lower trade costs favor core regions through stronger agglomeration forces, this relies on mobile labor. Under strong enough labor mobility, there would be greater spatial agglomeration and greater regional divergence (core-periphery effect). If labor remains relatively immobile between regions so that labor and congestion costs eventually rise in the core, spatial dispersion of economic activity will take place and regional convergence will appear. 

Nevertheless, given that population mobility plays a central role in the spatial Balassa-Samuelson effect, the results demonstrate that there exists a sufficient labor mobility to obtain the pattern we observe. 

\section{Conclusion}

This paper investigates the long-run impact of improved access to international markets—through reductions in internal transport costs—on urbanization and agricultural specialization in Brazil. Leveraging the Plan for National Integration (PIN) as a source of exogenous variation in road construction, I find robust evidence that micro-regions experiencing greater reductions in travel time to ports saw significantly higher growth in GDP and urban population between 1970 and 2010. These effects are persistent, emerge shortly after the start of construction, and intensify over time.

The findings align with the theoretical predictions of a spatial Ricardian trade model that features a Balassa-Samuelson mechanism. Improved market access leads to greater specialization in the tradable sector, raising incomes and shifting labor into the non-tradable sector. Specifically, road-connected regions specialized in more transport-cost-sensitive agricultural commodities like soybeans, with increases in production driven by both new entrants and existing producers. In contrast, transport-cost-insensitive goods such as cattle exhibited weaker or no responses.

Furthermore, the magnitude and nature of the effects vary across regions. The gains are disproportionately larger in peripheral areas with limited prior access to infrastructure, in regions without waterway substitutes, and in areas more distant from state capitals. These patterns suggest that the value of new infrastructure depends critically on regions' initial conditions.

Overall, infrastructure-led integration with world markets can act as a powerful force for structural transformation and spatial development. Future work could build on this framework to more precisely distinguish the channels through which internal and external integration interact and affect economic geography.
